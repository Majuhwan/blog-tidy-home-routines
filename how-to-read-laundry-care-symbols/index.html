How to Read Laundry Care Symbols, and Why They're a Federal Rule

What the FTC's Care Labeling Rule actually requires on clothing tags, and how to read the care symbols it allows manufacturers to use instead of words.

Tidy Home RoutinesOct 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Those small symbols sewn into the inside seam of a shirt aren't a styling choice. They exist because of a specific federal regulation, the Care Labeling Rule, enforced by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). Understanding what the rule actually requires makes the symbols far less mysterious, because every one of them exists to satisfy a specific legal obligation, not an arbitrary manufacturer preference.

Who has to follow the rule, and what's covered

The FTC's Care Labeling Rule applies to manufacturers and importers of textile wearing apparel, and separately to manufacturers and importers of piece goods sold to consumers for making their own apparel at home. It covers textile apparel worn to cover or protect the body, with specific exemptions: shoes, gloves, and hats are exempt, and items like handkerchiefs, belts, suspenders, neckties, and non-woven garments made for one-time use are excluded entirely. Piece goods sold for home sewing have their own exemptions, including small manufacturer remnants where the fiber content can't easily be determined, and narrow trim up to five inches wide.

What the label actually has to do

The core requirement, per the FTC, is that covered manufacturers and importers must provide complete instructions about regular care for the garment, or provide a clear warning if the garment can't be cleaned without harm. The label's instructions have to be reliable enough that, if followed exactly, they won't cause substantial harm to the product.

There's a specific nuance in the rule that catches people off guard: labels also have to warn about procedures a consumer might reasonably assume are fine, even if they're not explicitly mentioned. The FTC's own example is a pair of pants labeled for washing. A consumer might reasonably assume that garment can also be ironed. If ironing would actually damage it, the label is required to explicitly say "Do not iron," rather than relying on the consumer to somehow guess that ironing wasn't included in the general washing instructions.

"Reasonable basis" is a real legal standard

Manufacturers can't print any care instruction they feel like. The FTC requires a reasonable basis, meaning reliable evidence, for every instruction and warning on the label. A manufacturer can't print "Dryclean Only" without actually having proof that washing would harm the garment. The FTC notes that in some cases, industry experience and expertise can serve as that reasonable basis, but it has to be real evidence, not a guess made to be safe or to avoid liability.

This matters for how you should read a label in practice. A "Dryclean Only" instruction is not automatically the most cautious or protective option; under the rule, it's a specific, evidence-backed claim about what will and won't damage that particular fabric and construction.

Why some labels use symbols instead of words

The Care Labeling Rule allows manufacturers to use a specific symbol system in place of written words, as long as those symbols meet all the same substantive requirements as a worded label would. In the United States, the approved symbol system is the one from the American Society for Testing and Materials (ASTM), specifically ASTM Standard D5489-96c. The FTC notes that these ASTM symbols are similar to, but not identical to, the international symbol system used by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) in many European countries, which is one reason an imported garment's symbol-only tag can look slightly unfamiliar even when you already know the basic shapes.

Because only the ASTM symbols were officially approved for this purpose, a manufacturer using symbols rather than words is still bound by the exact same reasonable-basis and no-harm standards as a worded label; the symbol is a substitute presentation, not a loophole around the underlying requirements.

What some manufacturers do for garments that can be cleaned either way

The FTC's guidance notes that some manufacturers deliberately write instructions for both washing and drycleaning on the same garment, since surveys show many consumers specifically want washing instructions even when drycleaning is also an option. A common combined instruction is something like "may be washed, but for best results, dryclean," which tells you the garment survives either method, with drycleaning offering a cosmetic or durability edge rather than being strictly required.

Why the chlorine bleach symbol itself matters for safety, not just fabric

A care label's bleach symbol (whether it allows any bleach, chlorine bleach specifically, or none at all) connects directly to a separate safety concern worth knowing. The Washington State Department of Health warns that chlorine bleach, whose active ingredient is sodium hypochlorite, reacts dangerously with ammonia and with acidic products, producing toxic chloramine or chlorine gas that can cause coughing, watery eyes, chest pain, and in high enough exposure, fluid in the lungs. This matters at laundry time specifically because some stain removers, rust treatments, and even certain detergent boosters contain acids or ammonia, so following a garment's bleach instructions also means being careful about what else goes into the same wash water or pretreatment step, not just whether the garment's fabric can tolerate bleach.

A practical way to use this

The next time a care label seems overly cautious, remember that it's a specific legal claim backed by a reasonable-basis standard, not just a manufacturer covering itself. "Do not iron," "Dryclean Only," and similar hard instructions reflect actual testing or industry-standard knowledge about that fabric, not guesswork. Following them is less about superstition and more about trusting a federally required, evidence-based warning.

Key takeaways

  • The FTC's Care Labeling Rule legally requires manufacturers and importers of covered apparel to provide complete care instructions or warnings, with specific exemptions like shoes, gloves, hats, and small accessories.
  • Labels must warn about any care procedure a consumer might reasonably assume is safe but actually isn't, such as ironing a washable garment that can't tolerate heat.
  • Every care instruction or warning requires a "reasonable basis," meaning real evidence, not a cautious guess made to avoid liability.
  • Symbol-based labels in the US use the ASTM-approved symbol system, similar to but not identical to the international ISO symbols used in many other countries.
  • A garment labeled for both washing and drycleaning usually means washing is safe, with drycleaning offered as a cosmetic or durability improvement rather than a strict requirement.

A care label is a federally regulated claim about what will and won't damage a specific garment. Reading it that way makes the small print, and the small symbols, worth actually following.
